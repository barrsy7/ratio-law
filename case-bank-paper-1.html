Paper 1 — Criminal Law

Case law for AQA A-Level Law Paper 1: Criminal Law. Click a card to flip it and reveal the facts and point of law. Sourced from the AQA specification and examiner materials.

Murder

R v Vickers [1957]

Tap to reveal facts

D broke into an elderly shopkeeper's cellar to steal; when she disturbed him, he punched and kicked her and she died from her injuries.

Point of law: Intention to cause GBH is sufficient mens rea for murder — D doesn't need to have intended to kill.

R v Woollin [1998]

Tap to reveal facts

D threw his 3-month-old son onto a hard surface in frustration when the baby wouldn't stop crying; the baby died of a fractured skull.

Point of law: Established the modern “virtual certainty” test: a jury may find intention where death or GBH was virtually certain and D appreciated that.

R v White [1910]

Tap to reveal facts

D put cyanide in his mother's drink meaning to kill her for his inheritance, but she died of an unrelated heart attack before the poison could take effect.

Point of law: Factual causation (the ‘but for’ test) failed, so D was guilty only of attempted murder, not murder.

R v Smith [1959]

Tap to reveal facts

D stabbed a fellow soldier during a fight; the victim was dropped twice while being carried for treatment and given poor medical care, then died.

Point of law: The stab wound was still the operating and substantial cause of death — legal causation was satisfied.

R v Blaue [1975]

Tap to reveal facts

D stabbed a woman who was a Jehovah's Witness; she refused a blood transfusion on religious grounds and died from her injuries.

Point of law: The ‘thin skull’ rule: D must take his victim as he finds them, so her refusal didn't break the chain of causation.

R v Malcherek and Steel [1981]

Tap to reveal facts

Doctors switched off life support machines for two victims who were declared brain-dead after being attacked by the defendants.

Point of law: Turning off life support does not break the chain of causation from the defendant's original act.

Voluntary manslaughter

R v Clinton [2012]

Tap to reveal facts

D killed his wife during a temporary separation after she taunted him about her affairs and mocked his suicidal thoughts.

Point of law: Sexual infidelity alone can't be a qualifying trigger for loss of control, but it can be considered alongside other things said or done.

R v Dawes [2013]

Tap to reveal facts

D came home to find his wife with the victim on the sofa and stabbed him.

Point of law: A defendant can't rely on loss of control based on a situation he provoked himself, or on sexual infidelity alone.

DPP v Camplin [1978]

Tap to reveal facts

A 15-year-old boy killed a man who had mocked him after sexually assaulting him.

Point of law: The ‘reasonable person’ used to judge loss of control should share the defendant's age and sex.

R v Byrne [1960]

Tap to reveal facts

D, who described himself as suffering violent, uncontrollable sexual desires, strangled a young woman and mutilated her body.

Point of law: Established that ‘abnormality of mental functioning’ for diminished responsibility includes an inability to control one's physical acts, not just impaired reasoning.

R v Dietschmann [2003]

Tap to reveal facts

D killed the victim while suffering an abnormality of mental functioning (grief-related depression) and while also intoxicated.

Point of law: Diminished responsibility can still apply even if D was also drunk, as long as the abnormality substantially impaired his responsibility regardless of the drink.

Non-fatal offences

R v Ireland; R v Burstow [1998]

Tap to reveal facts

Ireland made repeated silent phone calls to women, causing psychiatric harm; Burstow carried out a stalking campaign that caused his ex-partner severe depression.

Point of law: Silence can amount to an assault, and psychiatric injury can amount to GBH under s20 — no physical contact is required.

R v Constanza [1997]

Tap to reveal facts

D sent around 800 threatening letters to a former colleague, causing her to fear for her safety.

Point of law: Words and letters can cause the fear of violence ‘at some time not excluding the immediate future’, which is enough for assault.

Collins v Wilcock [1984]

Tap to reveal facts

A police officer grabbed a woman's arm to stop her walking away, without arresting her.

Point of law: Any touching that goes beyond what's normally acceptable in everyday life can amount to a battery.

R v Savage; R v Parmenter [1992]

Tap to reveal facts

Savage threw beer over a woman and the glass slipped, cutting her; Parmenter handled his baby roughly, unaware it could cause injury, and it suffered fractures.

Point of law: For s47 (ABH) and s20 (GBH), the prosecution doesn't need to prove D foresaw that level of harm — only the mens rea for the underlying assault, or foresight of some harm.

R v Chan-Fook [1994]

Tap to reveal facts

D suspected his fiancée's brother of theft, locked him in a room and questioned him; the victim panicked and was injured jumping from a window to escape.

Point of law: ABH can include a recognisable psychiatric injury, but not mere fear, panic or other fleeting emotions.

Property offences

R v Gomez [1993]

Tap to reveal facts

D, a shop assistant, persuaded his manager to accept goods in exchange for cheques he knew were stolen and worthless.

Point of law: Appropriation can occur even where the owner consents, if that consent was obtained by deception.

R v Hinks [2000]

Tap to reveal facts

D befriended a man of limited intelligence and, over several months, got him to hand over £60,000 in cash gifts.

Point of law: Even a valid gift under civil law can still count as an appropriation for the purposes of theft.

Ivey v Genting Casinos [2017]

Tap to reveal facts

A professional gambler used a technique to gain an edge playing cards; the casino refused to pay his winnings, alleging he had cheated.

Point of law: Set the modern test for dishonesty — a single objective standard, replacing the old two-stage test from R v Ghosh.

R v Velumyl [1989]

Tap to reveal facts

A company director took money from the safe, intending to repay it once he received money he was owed.

Point of law: Intending to replace money later with different notes is still an intention to permanently deprive — he never meant to return the actual property taken.

R v Collins [1972]

Tap to reveal facts

D climbed a ladder to a woman's bedroom window at night intending to have sex with her without consent; she invited him in, believing he was her boyfriend, just before he was fully inside.

Point of law: Entry as a trespasser requires D to know or be reckless as to being a trespasser at the point of ‘effective and substantial’ entry.

R v Dawson and James [1976]

Tap to reveal facts

Three defendants jostled and pushed a man while one of them tried to take his wallet from his pocket.

Point of law: Any degree of force, however slight, is enough to satisfy the force element of robbery.
